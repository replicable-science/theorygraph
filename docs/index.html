The Theory Graph

What theories of value do founders actually hold, and are they built to be revised one part at a time? The theory-based view says entrepreneurs should hold and test such theories, but what founders hold has been out of view. The theory graph uses AI to read them from founders’ own words: 375 pitches so far, every element traceable to the passage it comes from.

Read it left to right. Each premise is something the founder treats as necessary for the venture to create value. Premises that would have to be revised together form a strategic domain, and the domains feed the value the pitch promises.

In four lines.

  • The question. The theory-based view casts entrepreneurs as theorists, and training experiments show that testing a theory helps. But what theories do founders hold untrained? How broad are they, and are they near-decomposable (Simon, 1962): built so that one part can be revised without rebuilding the whole?
  • What AI makes possible. Startup Battlefield is a rare record: 375 founders (2009–2023) each state a whole value theory in the same six-minute format to expert judges, and what became of the venture is visible years later. A language model reads each pitch into a theory graph with a fixed codebook, three times. By hand this stops at a few cases.
  • First answers. Theories are modular: 99% span two or more strategic domains. Two features of their architecture go with survival: breadth, with survival highest at about 10 premises, and clearer separation between domains. Both are exploratory: hypotheses for Stage 2.
  • Why trust it. Every element quotes its source passage, which is checked against the transcript; every reading is repeated; a human benchmark follows in Stage 2. Code, prompts and derived data are open, and one command rebuilds every number on this page.

1. What becomes observable

The theory-based view holds that entrepreneurs who form and test theories of value make better decisions, and field experiments that train founders to do so support it (Camuffo et al., 2020; Agarwal et al., 2023). What untrained founders’ theories look like has stayed out of view. They are implicit and surface mainly in how founders talk, and coding them by hand does not scale beyond a few ventures. A language model can read a founder’s words with a fixed codebook, quote the passage behind each element, and do so for thousands of ventures. A construct that has been theorised becomes one that can be measured.

What we measureWhy it matters for theory
Premises and breadthWhat a founder treats as necessary for value creation, and how many: narrow theories leave blind spots, broad ones are hard to test.
Strategic domainsWhich premises would be revised together if one failed. Near-decomposable theories (Simon) allow modular, targeted learning.
Stated links (Stage 2)Which premise the founder says depends on which: the causal structure as the founder asserts it, not as we estimate it.
Evidential status (Stage 2)Whether a premise is backed by evidence, planned, marked as uncertain, or only asserted: where the theory has been tested and where it is a bet.
Change (Stage 2)Which premises are kept, qualified or dropped as evidence arrives, read from a venture’s dated updates: learning, observed.
Challenge (Stage 2)Which premises the judges question in the Q&A, and how founders respond: how a theory holds up under scrutiny.

The architecture of a value theory

A theory graph is hierarchical: premises come first, strategic domains group them, and the structure ends in the value the venture promises. A six-minute pitch yields one layer of domains, because there is little room to say more. In a separate analysis of the same pitches, the domains fall into four broader areas: the customer problem or pain point, the product mechanism, the money module, and the route to market. Longer texts, such as a venture’s dated updates, can support further layers, so the same instrument can show how deep a founder’s theory goes.

The questions we start with. How broad is a founder’s theory, and how broad should it be? A narrow theory leaves conditions for value unexamined; a sprawling one is hard to test. And is it near-decomposable, in Simon’s sense: do premises depend on each other mainly within strategic domains, so that a failed premise forces a local revision rather than a new theory? And are ventures with more decomposable theories more likely to survive, as a theory of learning would predict? Section 3 gives first answers from the premises and domains; Stage 2 adds the dependencies founders state and the evidence they cite for each premise.

What we read from it. Survival rises with breadth up to about 10 premises and falls beyond. The rise is well covered by the data; the fall rests on few theories (5 pitches have 13 or more premises). Premise counts are also the most model-dependent measure (section 4), so this is a reading by GPT-4o mini, to be checked against the human benchmark in Stage 2.

Are founders’ theories near-decomposable?

Simon (1962) argued that complex systems that can adapt are near-decomposable: strong interactions within parts, weak ones between them. For a value theory this means premises hang together mainly inside a strategic domain, so a failed premise forces revision of its domain, not of the whole theory. We measure how clearly a theory separates into domains: the mean similarity of premises within a domain minus between domains. Premises within a domain are more similar than premises across domains in 82% of pitches. We then ask whether clearer separation goes with survival, with the same sample, outcome and controls as Table 2; separation is standardised.

What we read from it. Ventures whose theories separate more cleanly into domains were more likely to stay open (odds ratio 1.35 per standard deviation), and this does not change when breadth and breadth squared or the number of domains are added. The association is weaker with an embedding-based measure of separation and rests on 72 closures. We treat it as a hypothesis for Stage 2, not a finding. The direct test of near-decomposability, whether the dependencies founders state stay inside domains, needs the dependency pass on all pitches (Stage 2).

Next: evidence and the judges’ questions. Two further passes are built and tested on three fictional pitches in examples/, so the pipeline can be replayed without using a real transcript: one codes how each premise is supported and which dependencies the founder states; one records which premise each of the judges’ questions tests and how the founders respond. Running them on all pitches is Stage 2 work.

4. Can the measurement be trusted?

A language model always returns a theory graph. The call asks when AI-enabled research is valid science and when it builds “Potemkin villages”. We answer that for our own instrument with three checks.

Check 1: is every element anchored in what the founder said?

For every premise the model must copy a passage from the transcript, and we check that the passage is there (after lower-casing and removing punctuation). Of 8,728 passages, 8,439 are found word for word, 183 with small differences, and 106 are not found and flagged. The check catches invented quotes; it does not show that a real passage was read correctly, which is what Check 3 is for.

Check 2: run again, does it give the same theory?

Yes. Each pitch was read three times. The number of premises per pitch is reliable across runs (Krippendorff’s α = 0.81 for one run, 0.93 for the median of three), and the grouping of premises into domains reproduces closely (adjusted Rand index 0.85, 95% interval 0.82 to 0.87; 0 is chance, 1 is identical).

Check 3: is it close to trained human coders? (Stage 2)

Two trained coders will code about 40 random pitches independently, following a written protocol (benchmark/PROTOCOL.md). Their agreement sets the ceiling for the model, and the human sample corrects regressions that use model-coded variables.

Robustness: other models read the same pitches differently

We also read the 375 pitches with Grok 4.7, Claude and Qwen3-32B. Each is stable with itself, but models differ from each other: premise counts correlate 0.14 to 0.38 across models, and two models assigning the same premises to the same ten labels agree at 0.11 (adjusted Rand index; shuffled baseline 0.00). A theory graph is therefore reported with the model that produced it, and the human benchmark decides which reading is closest to trained coders.

5. Next steps and limits

Stage 2. (0) Test near-decomposability properly: run the dependency pass on all 375 pitches, pre-register the survival model, and check in a venture’s dated updates whether revisions stay within a domain. (1) Run the human benchmark and report the model against it. (2) Test stronger and open-weight models on the same protocol. (3) Code the judges’ questions for all 375 pitches. (4) Attempt the full roster of about 1,700 Battlefield pitches, as far as recordings can be obtained. (5) Apply the pipeline to a long text series, Buffer’s public shareholder updates, to observe how a theory changes.

Limits we state up front.

  • No human benchmark yet. Until it exists we know the reading is anchored and stable, not that it matches trained coders; other models read pitches differently (section 4).
  • Pitches are persuasion. They show espoused theories, not private beliefs; the judges’ questions show how those theories hold up.
  • The prompts for status, links and challenge are a first version, written on three fictional pitches.
  • Pitches are short and flat (7.7 premises on average, one level of domains). Deeper structure needs longer sources.
  • Transcripts come from speech recognition without speaker labels; transcription errors pass through to the coding.
  • The survival result is exploratory. One association, 72 closures on TechCrunch’s listing (73 of 375 ventures), observational, and weaker with an embedding-based measure. The listing can miss closures.
  • Public prompts differ from the as-run prompts only in leaving out three third-party transcripts used as worked examples; prompts/README.md gives the hash of each.

7. Data, ethics and use of AI

  • Shared: the codings of 375 pitches (premise statements, groupings, quote checks, similarity measures), value statements, each venture’s TechCrunch status, the prompts and their hashes, the three fictional pitches with every model reply, code, and tests.
  • Not shared: transcripts, quoted passages of real pitches, and Crunchbase fields (exact dates, funding), which are licensed. Public video links and cut times (data/video_sources.csv) plus data/rebuild_transcripts.py let anyone with the right to process the videos rebuild transcripts.
  • People. Pitches are public performances at a startup competition. We do not code or release any characteristic of the presenters. Company names appear only to identify the public recording.
  • Risks we see. Mislabeling a founder’s reasoning in a way that travels as fact; we therefore show every output beside its quote and its check, and LIMITATIONS.md separates what is measured from what is only planned.
  • Use of AI. A language model does the reading under study, and that is what is checked. A language-model coding assistant (in Cursor) helped write the code and the text of this page.
